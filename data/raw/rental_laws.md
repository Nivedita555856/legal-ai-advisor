# Rental & Accommodation Laws in India

## Governing Laws:
- Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (Section 105-117)
- State Rent Control Acts (Maharashtra Rent Control Act 1999, Delhi Rent Control Act 1958, etc.)
- Specific Relief Act, 1963 (Section 6)
- Electricity Act, 2003
- Constitutional: Article 14, Article 21

---

## Key Rights of a Tenant

### 1. Verbal Agreements are Legally Valid
Under Section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, a monthly tenancy can be created without a written agreement. If rent is paid and accepted via UPI, bank transfer, or cash for consecutive months, a valid tenancy exists.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a rent agreement does not need to be on stamp paper to be legally valid.

Key case: Gulabbai vs. Nalin Narsi Vohra (1991) — court recognized tenancy without written agreement based on payment receipts.

Practical tip: UPI receipts, bank statements showing monthly rent payment are sufficient proof of tenancy. Collect and save all payment records.

### 2. Landlord Cannot Call You a Trespasser Without Due Process
If you have been paying rent (even without a written agreement) and the landlord suddenly files a police complaint calling you a "trespasser," you can:
1. Produce payment receipts as evidence
2. Invoke Section 6 of the Specific Relief Act (possession suit) — you can file a summary suit to regain possession if wrongfully dispossessed within 6 months
3. Cite Article 14 — arbitrary treatment violates the right to equality

### 3. Landlord Cannot Cut Off Utilities
Cutting off electricity or water supply to force a tenant to vacate is:
- A criminal offense under Section 163 of the Electricity Act, 2003
- Violation of Article 21 (Right to Life includes right to basic amenities)
- Actionable under the Consumer Protection Act 2019 (deficiency of service)

What to do: File a police complaint at the local police station. File a complaint with the electricity distribution company. File a complaint before the State Electricity Regulatory Commission.

### 4. Notice Period Before Eviction
Under Section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act:
- For monthly tenancy: 15 days written notice required
- For annual tenancy: 6 months written notice required

Most State Rent Control Acts require even longer notice periods (1-3 months) and mandate court permission for eviction.

A landlord CANNOT simply lock the door. They must:
1. Issue a formal written notice to vacate
2. File an eviction suit in the Rent Control Court
3. Get a court order for eviction
4. Have the court order executed by a bailiff

### 5. Rent Increase Rules
Under most Rent Control Acts, a landlord cannot increase rent by more than a fixed percentage per year (typically 4-10%) without the tenant's consent or a court order.

## Landlord Rights

### Grounds for Eviction (Bona Fide Need):
A landlord can seek eviction if:
1. They genuinely need the property for personal use (bona fide need)
2. The tenant has not paid rent for 2+ months
3. The property is being used for illegal activities
4. The tenant has sublet without permission
5. The tenant has caused structural damage

Key case: Bega Begum vs Abdul Ahad Khan (1979) — bona fide need must be genuine, not pretextual.

---

## Practical Steps for Tenants Facing Harassment

1. Document everything: Save all payment receipts, messages, emails
2. Send a legal notice: Via registered post citing Section 106 TPA and Article 21
3. File a police complaint: If utilities are cut or threats are made
4. Approach Rent Control Court: File a petition for protection against eviction
5. File a complaint with District Magistrate: For illegal eviction attempts
